02 · The basics

What is
Ausbildung?

"Ausbildung" is simply the German word for training. It's the country's dual vocational education system, and for the right person it's a faster, cheaper and more practical route into Germany than university.

Lernort 1BerufsschuleVocational school: the theory behind your trade

Lernort 2AusbildungsbetriebYour training company: real, hands-on work, paid monthly
01

What "Ausbildung" means

Germany's dual vocational training system: practical, job-specific training instead of a broad academic degree.

02

Vocational training

You train for a specific, recognised profession: nursing, IT, mechatronics, hospitality and 300+ others.

03

Classroom + practical work

Split between a Berufsschule (vocational school) and real, hands-on work at your training company.

04

Paid every month

You're an employee from day one, with a real contract and a monthly training salary: the Ausbildungsvergütung.

05

Usually 2–3.5 years

Exact duration depends on the profession: retail can be shorter, technical trades like mechatronics run longer.

06

A recognised qualification

You finish with a nationally recognised qualification (an IHK or HWK certificate; nursing ends with a state exam), trusted by German employers.

03 · Fit check

Is Ausbildung
right for you?

Neither path is "better". They're for different people. Be honest with yourself about which one actually fits.

✓ Suitable if you…
  • Don't want to spend 3–5 years in university
  • Want hands-on, practical work over classroom theory
  • Want to earn a salary from day one, not spend on tuition
  • Prefer learning by doing rather than by studying alone
  • Want a direct, stable path to long-term employment in Germany
✕ Not suitable if you…
  • Are aiming for academic research or a PhD track
  • Want to go straight into a Master's programme
  • Don't enjoy hands-on, practical work
  • Need a role that specifically requires a university degree
04 · Getting ready

What
you need.

Eligibility, language level and paperwork: everything to check off before you apply.

School qualification

Usually 10–12 years of schooling depending on the trade. Technical and healthcare programmes often prefer 12 years.

Age

No official upper age limit for the training visa, but most employers prefer candidates roughly 18–30. The separate visa to search for a training place is only for applicants under 35.

Financial proof

Waived if your training salary is high enough: for 2026 the threshold is €1,048 gross (≈ €822 net) per month. If it's lower, a blocked account or a sponsor's declaration of commitment covers the gap. Full detail →

Motivation

Genuine interest in the trade: the single most-checked signal in interviews. Generic applications get filtered out fast.

Visa

A residence permit for vocational training (Aufenthaltserlaubnis zur Berufsausbildung, §16a), different from the student visa.

Health insurance

Mandatory from day one, largely handled through statutory payroll deductions once you start.

Not sure you qualify?

Work through every requirement properly before you apply. Ausbildung Eligibility Checklist →

A2The rare exception

A handful of programmes accept A2 with extra language support built in, but these remain the exception, not the rule.

B1The realistic minimum

The training visa normally asks for B1, and most employers and Berufsschulen expect at least B1. This covers retail, logistics, office administration and most hospitality roles.

B2Technical & healthcare roles

Nursing, healthcare and technical trades with heavy customer or patient interaction typically expect B2. Check the field cards above for specifics.

  5. 05

  6. 06

    See the full visa process →
  7. 07
    Step 7 · Travel

    Arrange accommodation and fly to Germany

    Sort your first weeks of accommodation before you land, register your address (Anmeldung) within two weeks of moving in, and activate your health insurance.

  8. 08
    Step 8 · Start

    Begin your Ausbildung

    You're now an Azubi: employed, paid, and training toward a recognised German qualification. Many trainees are offered a job by their training company when they finish.

Gross → net

These figures are gross. In 2026 your share of social insurance (health, long-term care, pension, unemployment) is roughly 21%, while most trainees pay little or no income tax on a training salary. Basic health insurance is already deducted from your gross pay rather than an extra cost.

Can you live on it?

A realistic monthly budget (shared flat, food, trainee-discount transport, phone, some leisure) lands around €700–900. Yes, you can live on it, but it's tight in year one, especially in expensive cities like Munich or Hamburg, and it gets easier by year two as pay rises. Most trainees share a flat, and some employers offer subsidised housing or meals: ask before you sign. Compare offers, since pay varies by employer, and expect to cover your costs, not build savings, in year one.

07 · Once you're ready

The application
playbook.

Where to search, how the visa works, and what trips people up, for when you're ready to actually apply.

Apply broadly, across multiple portals, rather than waiting on one application at a time.

Once your contract is signed, the visa is mostly paperwork and patience. You apply online through the Consular Services Portal at the German mission for your state; waiting times vary. A Federal Employment Agency pre-approval isn't mandatory but can shorten processing, and your employer can use the fast-track procedure (€411).

  2. Financial proofWaived if your pay is at least €1,048 gross
  3. AccommodationProof of first-weeks housing
  4. InsuranceHealth cover active before travel
  5. EmbassyApplication, documents, interview
  6. TravelFly, register your address, begin

  • Applying with weak German. B1 on paper isn't the same as speaking confidently in an interview. Weak spoken German is the #1 rejection reason.
  • Generic CV. A CV that isn't formatted the German way (tabellarischer Lebenslauf, photo, clear structure) gets filtered before it's even read.
  • Poor motivation letter. Copy-pasted, generic letters are obvious to employers who read hundreds of them. Be specific about why this trade, why this company.
  • Applying too late. Most training years start in August or September, and many companies fill places months earlier. Late applicants get the leftover spots.
  • Wrong expectations. Expecting a €3,000+ salary from day one, or thinking Ausbildung is "easier" than it is. It's real, paid work: treat it that way.
  • No interview preparation. Walking into a video interview without practising spoken German or researching the company is the most avoidable mistake on this list.
