Learn words you'll actually use

Learning German gets much easier once you stop memorizing random words and start with vocabulary you'll genuinely use — this pack focuses on high-frequency A1 words for everyday communication, plus the situations international students and newcomers commonly run into in Germany.

Learn the article with every noun. Don't learn Wohnung = apartment. Learn die Wohnung = apartment. That small habit pays off enormously once you reach German grammar.
der (masculine) die (feminine) das (neuter)

This color coding runs through every table below — the more you see it, the more it sticks.

Recommended method
  • Learn 10–15 words at a time
  • Learn the article with every noun
  • Say each word aloud
  • Read the example sentence
  • Create your own sentence
  • Review yesterday's words before learning new ones
  • Practise using the words in speaking, not just recognizing them
  • Mark difficult words and review them again

Now the sentences

Words on their own get you surprisingly far, and then they stop. Everything below is the other half: what to actually say at the counter, on the phone, at the Bürgeramt and in the waiting room — the German a classroom tends not to cover because it is not grammar, it is just what people say.

Start here

German for situations a classroom never covers

Moving to Germany means using German in situations you may never have practiced in a classroom. You may know hundreds of German words and still struggle when someone asks you a question at the supermarket, a receptionist answers the phone quickly, a train announcement changes, or you need to explain something at the Bürgeramt.

This guide focuses on complete phrases you can actually use — for:

ShoppingRestaurantsPublic transport UniversityWorkHousing AppointmentsDoctors & pharmaciesBanks Phone callsBehördenAsking for clarification Everyday conversationHandling problems
Tip: Don't try to memorize the entire guide at once. Pick the phrases relevant to your current situation and start using them.

The Goethe-Institut's own A1/A2 materials take the same practical approach — emphasizing introductions, shopping, restaurants, directions and routine interactions over abstract vocabulary. See Official Sources ↓.

Category 2

Du or Sie?

A simple rule: when you're unsure in a formal or professional situation, Sie is the safer starting point. Use du with people you already have an informal relationship with, such as friends and many fellow students. At university and workplaces, conventions can differ — pay attention to what the other person uses.

GermanEnglish
Können wir uns duzen?Can we use "du"?
Wollen wir uns duzen?Shall we use "du"?
Gerne.Sure / gladly.
Sie können mich gerne duzen.You can use "du" with me.
Bleiben wir beim Sie?Shall we continue using "Sie"?
German LifeBox Tip: If you're starting a new university job, internship or professional relationship, don't assume du. Let the context guide you.

The Goethe-Institut treats du/Sie as a contextual communication skill, not just a vocabulary distinction — see "May I Say 'Du' to You?" ↗

The real shortcut

Useful sentence patterns

Instead of memorizing 150 unrelated sentences, learn reusable patterns. One pattern can become dozens of useful sentences.

Ich hätte gerne + noun
Ich hätte gerne einen Kaffee.
I'd like a coffee.
Ich hätte gerne ein Ticket nach München.
I'd like a ticket to Munich.
Wo finde ich + noun?
Wo finde ich die Kasse?
Where can I find the checkout?
Wo finde ich das Studierendensekretariat?
Where can I find the student office?
Ich möchte + infinitive/noun
Ich möchte einen Termin vereinbaren.
I'd like to make an appointment.
Ich möchte ein Girokonto eröffnen.
I'd like to open a current account.
Können Sie bitte + verb?
Können Sie bitte warten?
Could you please wait?
Können Sie das bitte wiederholen?
Could you please repeat that?
Ich habe eine Frage zu + noun
Ich habe eine Frage zu meiner Rechnung.
I have a question about my bill.
Ich habe eine Frage zu dieser Aufgabe.
I have a question about this assignment.
Ich suche + noun
Ich suche eine Wohnung.
I'm looking for an apartment.
Ich suche den Bahnhof.
I'm looking for the station.

From experience

German LifeBox tip

👨‍🎓
Bibin Johnson

Don't wait until your German is perfect before speaking German. You don't need a huge vocabulary to handle everyday situations — learn a few complete phrases, use them repeatedly, and gradually swap out individual words inside the sentence.

Ich hätte gerne … → Ich hätte gerne einen Kaffee. → Ich hätte gerne ein Ticket. → Ich hätte gerne einen Termin. → Ich hätte gerne die Rechnung.

One sentence pattern can become dozens of useful sentences. That's genuinely how I got through my own first months here.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What German vocabulary should I learn at A1?+
Start with greetings, personal information, numbers, time, family, food, shopping, housing, transport and everyday communication — the categories in this pack, roughly in that order.
Should I learn German nouns with their articles?+
Yes. Learn der/die/das together with each noun, rather than memorizing the English translation on its own — it makes later German grammar much easier.
How many German words do I need for A1?+
There's no universal CEFR rule for an exact word count. The Goethe-Institut publishes its own official vocabulary list for the Goethe-Zertifikat A1 exam — the closest thing to an authoritative benchmark.
Is this the official Goethe A1 vocabulary list?+
No. This is a German LifeBox practical vocabulary resource, built around everyday communication and newcomer needs — not a reproduction of Goethe's exam word list.
What German words are particularly useful when moving to Germany?+
Words like Wohnung, Mietvertrag, Bürgeramt, Anmeldung, Krankenkasse, Mensa, Fahrkarte, Girokonto and Ausländerbehörde — covered in the Germany-Specific Survival Vocabulary section.
Is A1 German enough to live in Germany?+
A1 helps with simple everyday situations, but isn't enough for independent communication in most complex ones. Make it in Germany recommends stronger German for things like shopping, the foreigners authority, and doctor visits independently.
Can I use this vocabulary pack to prepare for the Goethe A1 exam?+
It can support your prep, but shouldn't replace Goethe's own official practice material or vocabulary list — they provide free listening, reading, writing and speaking practice for exactly that.
Do I need advanced German to use these phrases?+
No. The guide focuses primarily on practical A1–A2-style communication, while some phrases naturally extend into B1-level everyday use.
Should I always use "Sie" with strangers?+
Not always. Context matters. In formal situations, Sie is a safe starting point, but German-speaking environments increasingly vary in their use of du and Sie.
What should I say if I don't understand someone?+
Use: Entschuldigung, ich habe das nicht verstanden. Können Sie bitte langsamer sprechen?
Is "Ich hätte gerne…" polite?+
Yes. It's a very common and useful way to express what you would like, especially when ordering.
Should I memorize every phrase?+
No. Start with phrases relevant to your current life in Germany and gradually expand your phrase bank.
Are these phrases only for students?+
No. They're useful for students, Ausbildung participants, workers, job seekers and other newcomers.
